Christopher Arledge

I work in the philosophy of physics and the philosophy of science. My research asks what formal results, from derivations in gravitational physics to criteria for statistical model selection, actually establish.

I received my Ph.D. in Philosophy from Johns Hopkins University in 2025, with a dissertation on the foundations of emergent gravity advised by Robert Rynasiewicz and Sean Carroll. I teach philosophy of physics at the University of Maryland Baltimore County and work as a data scientist and machine learning engineer.

Research

Some of the most interesting claims in philosophy rest on formal results from the sciences: that spacetime is emergent, that the laws are deterministic, that simpler models predict better. In each case the conclusion is only as good as the derivation behind it. I locate the assumptions that carry the weight and ask what follows once they are explicit. Usually the result is not wrong. It is narrower, more conditional, or about something different from what is advertised, and that difference changes the philosophical conclusion.

Thermodynamic gravity and emergence

Since Jacobson's 1995 derivation of the Einstein equation from the thermodynamics of causal horizons, many physicists have read general relativity as an equation of state: a coarse-grained description of unknown microscopic degrees of freedom, autonomous in the way fluid dynamics is autonomous from molecular dynamics. I argue the derivations do not support that reading as it stands. Once the corrections identified by Casini, Galante and Myers are included in Jacobson's 2016 entanglement-equilibrium argument, the recovered geometry responds to physics across many scales, so gravity is emergent in a more scale-dependent sense than the hydrodynamic analogy suggests. A related paper argues that the thermodynamic route yields the trace-free (unimodular) field equations rather than general relativity, with consequences for the cosmological constant problem.

Higher-curvature gravity, determinism, and laws

Philosophers of physics have made determinism precise as well-posedness of the initial value problem. General relativity is well-posed, but quantum corrections are expected to add higher powers of curvature, and recent results show that such theories are well-posed only when the extra terms are small, and in general only with auxiliary fields the theory itself treats as unphysical. Black holes in these theories satisfy a second law only within effective field theory. I argue that determinism and black hole thermodynamics hold within a regime of validity rather than of a theory as a whole, and that black hole thermodynamics is genuine but effective.

Model selection and simplicity

Criteria such as AIC, BIC and cross-validation are often defended as neutral razors that prefer simpler models without presupposing anything about the answer. I argue that each encodes assumptions about how the data were generated, and that its preference for simplicity is warranted only where those assumptions hold. Singular learning theory suggests complexity is a relation between a model and the process it describes, not a property of the model alone. A further paper treats uniqueness theorems such as Lovelock's as an unacknowledged step of model selection.

Book in progress

What the Derivations Deliver develops the thermodynamic gravity work into a single argument: the thermodynamic and entanglement derivations of gravity yield trace-free gravity rather than general relativity, scale-dependence rather than autonomy, and conditionals rather than derivations.

Papers

  1. Multi-Scale Entanglement and the Autonomy of Thermodynamic Gravity

    Under review

    Argues that once higher-order corrections to entanglement entropy are included, Jacobson's entanglement-equilibrium derivation recovers a geometry sensitive to physics at many scales, undermining the autonomy that makes the hydrodynamic picture of gravity attractive.

  2. When Is a Singularity Really Gone? Ontology, Incompleteness, and Lorentz-Violating Gravity

    Under review

    Examines what it takes for a theory to resolve a spacetime singularity, using gravitational theories without Lorentz invariance as a test case.

    Draft (PDF)

  3. Simplifying to Understand: An Indirect Epistemic Role for Simplicity in Astrophysical and Cosmological Modeling

    Under review

    Debates about simplicity usually treat it either as a reason to believe a theory or as a mere convenience. I identify a third role: a simplifying assumption known to be false can yield an interpretable relation among physical quantities that then guides more realistic models. The account is developed through Maxwell's 1855 fluid analogy and 1860 kinetic theory, Stäckel potentials in galactic dynamics, and the tight-coupling approximation in cosmological perturbation theory.

    Draft (PDF)

Dissertation

From Chaos to Order: Assessing the Foundations of Emergent Gravity (Johns Hopkins, 2025). Traces emergent gravity back to Newton, defends black hole thermodynamics against recent skepticism by appeal to the Unruh and Hawking effects, argues that thermodynamic gravity is a case of emergence consistent with reduction, and argues that in holographic gravity, gravity emerges from multi-scale entanglement.

Teaching

Many of my students are science, engineering and computer science majors who arrive doubting that philosophy has anything to offer them. I ask them to use their technical training rather than set it aside, introducing only as much mathematics as an argument needs, so that humanities students can follow the reasoning while science majors are pushed to say what their equations mean.
